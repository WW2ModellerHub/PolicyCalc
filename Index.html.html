How the proposed Council Tax reform could work

R1Flat 0.48% on value

Council tax is scrapped and replaced by a single annual charge of 0.48% of a property's current market value — the rate calibrated to raise the same total revenue as the taxes it replaces.

R2Owners pay, not tenants

Liability moves from the occupier to the property owner. Around 8.7 million renting households would stop receiving a council tax bill entirely.

R3Stamp duty abolished on main homes

Stamp Duty Land Tax goes for primary residences, removing the up-front cost of moving. It's retained for second homes and overseas buyers.

R4Double rate for second & empty homes

Second homes, long-term empty homes and homes owned by non-residents pay a 0.96% surcharge rate — double the standard charge.

R5£1,200 transitional cap

Existing owner-occupiers can't see their bill rise by more than £1,200 a year above what they pay today. The cap is removed when the property is sold — the new owner pays the full 0.48%.

R6Deferral for the asset-rich, cash-poor

Owners who can't afford the charge — for example pensioners in high-value homes — could defer payment, typically settled when the property changes hands.

Council Tax reform calculator FAQs

Q1What does this calculator compare?

It compares the council tax amount you enter with an estimated annual Proportional Property Tax based on the property value and scenario selected.

Q2Is Proportional Property Tax already law?

No. This page models a policy proposal for illustration. The final design, rate and protections could change if legislation were ever introduced.

Q3Why should I enter my exact council tax bill?

Council tax varies by local authority. Entering your actual annual charge produces a more relevant comparison than relying on a national band estimate.

Q4Does the calculator cover Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland?

No. The assumptions in this tool are designed for England only.

About this calculator. PolicyCalc models the Fairer Share Proportional Property Tax proposal. It is a proposal, not law, and any future legislation could differ.
